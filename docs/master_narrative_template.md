## The study in one page

**Question.** When fuel prices rise in the Philippines, do poor households pay more than everyone else, and does it depend on where they live?

**Data.** Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA) consumer price indices for all income households and for the bottom 30% income households, monthly, national and 17 regions, January 2000/1994 to August 2026; the CPI sub-index for retail motor fuels as the fuel price shock; world crude prices, exchange rate, policy rate, world food prices, poverty statistics; and EU data for validation.

**Method.** The local projection model of Kpodar and Liu (2022), validated by reproducing their published EU results before any Philippine estimate was made.

**Main findings.**

- A 1% rise in fuel prices raises Philippine prices by {{ n.rq1_all_c0 }} pp within a month and {{ n.rq1_all_c12 }} pp within a year; crude prices capture only about {{ n.crude_share }}% of this.
- Unlike the cross-country evidence, the effect is not progressive: in the regional panel, the poor's prices rise {{ n.rq4_c6|replace("−","") }} pp more after six months. The reason is larger price increases within categories (food, utilities), not the poor's spending pattern. The effect was strongest in 2001–2012.
- Since 2001, the poor's prices rose {{ n.d_cum_gap_ph|replace("−","") }} log points more than the average; in August 2026 their inflation was {{ n.yoy_b30_aug }}% against {{ n.yoy_all_aug }}%.
- A model estimated to 2025 explains about half of the 2026 surge; the fuel-driven cost for a poverty-line family of five reached about PHP {{ n.e3_ph_cost }} a month; rice drove most of the poor's excess inflation in 2026.
- Policy: gasoline and diesel tax relief mainly helps the better-off; LPG relief is distribution-neutral; transfers sized with the bottom-30% CPI, fare policy and rice policy are better targeted.

# Part II. The process, step by step

## 1. Starting point: the concept note and the reference study

The study began with your concept note (Appendix A), which set out four research questions, the target journal and the plan to replicate Kpodar and Liu (2021/2022). The first task was to read the reference paper (IMF Working Paper 21/271) closely enough to implement it exactly. Four details of the specification are not stated in its text and had to be inferred from its annex tables; each became an entry in the decision log:

- **D1 — the outcome is one month's inflation at horizon *h*, not the cumulative change.** In Annex Table 1, the coefficient on the lagged inflation term that falls in the same calendar month a year earlier stays near 0.47 as *h* changes (lag 12 at *h* = 0, lag 11 at *h* = 1, and so on). That pattern only appears if the outcome is a single month. Consequence: per-horizon coefficients are effects on monthly inflation; level effects are cumulative sums.
- **D2 — the data are not seasonally adjusted.** The large lag-12 coefficient shows seasonality absorbed by the lags.
- **D3 — a linear trend and a constant are included** with the fixed effects (both appear in the tables).
- **D4 — the Teulings–Zubanov terms are the fuel shocks at leads 1 to *h* − 1.** "Lead 1" first appears in the *h* = 2 column.

The reference values were transcribed into `replication/kpodar_liu_2021_targets.csv` so the replication could be checked against them.

## 2. Setting up the environment

The work was done in a cloud environment with a Git repository (`claude/bold-hamilton-0ixcim`). Two practical issues arose at the start:

- **Network access.** The environment initially blocked the statistical agencies' websites (PSA, World Bank, IMF, BSP). You changed the network setting to full access, after which all sources were reachable.
- **Software.** R was not installed and its package server was initially blocked, while Python worked. The analysis was therefore written in Python (pandas, statsmodels, linearmodels for panels with Driscoll–Kraay errors, matplotlib, pandoc for documents). Every package version is recorded in `requirements.txt`.

The repository layout follows the concept note: raw data, processed data, scripts, output, paper, plus a decision log in `CLAUDE.md`.

## 3. Data collection

Data were collected by scripts, never by hand, so that every file can be downloaded again. The search and its outcomes, including what did not work:

1. **PSA price indices.** The PSA's OpenSTAT database has a public API (PxWeb). Browsing it showed that the bottom-30% CPI exists back to January 2000 on the 2018 base — far longer than feared (the concept note's main feasibility question) — and that a CPI sub-index for fuels and lubricants for personal transport exists for every region from 1994. The script `01_download_psa.py` downloads 19 tables (both baskets, all base years, weights), one geography at a time to stay within the API's size limit.
   - *Problem 1:* some tables are served in Latin-1 rather than UTF-8 encoding; the first run crashed. Fixed by falling back to Latin-1.
   - *Problem 2:* one table labels Region XII as "Reg12" and MIMAROPA as "MIMAROPA Region", so two regions were silently skipped. Detected by counting regions per file (17 instead of 19); fixed by broadening the label pattern; the two tables were downloaded again.
2. **The reference study's fuel database** (Kpodar–Abdallah Global Monthly Retail Fuel Price Database) turned out not to be publicly downloadable.
3. **World Bank Global Fuel Prices Database:** downloaded, but it covers only December 2015–April 2025, misses the 2026 shock, and has no regions. Used for validation and robustness.
4. **IMF CPI database:** reachable through its API, but it has only COICOP divisions (no fuel sub-index) and only from 2010, so it could not replace the fuel database.
5. **World Bank Pink Sheet** (Dubai and Brent crude), **BSP** peso–dollar rate, **FAO** Food Price Index: downloaded from official URLs.
6. **BSP policy rate:** the BSP's own files could not be located at predictable URLs; the BIS central bank policy rate database (which compiles BSP data) was used instead.
7. **EU data for validation:** Eurostat harmonised CPI (all items and fuels), the European Commission Weekly Oil Bulletin (pump prices with and without taxes since 2005) and the euro–dollar rate.
8. **PSA poverty statistics** (2018, 2021, 2023 regional poverty incidence and thresholds), added for the regional analysis and peso costs.

Every raw file is listed with its URL, date, base and coverage in the source log (Appendix C). Raw PSA files were compressed so the repository stays small enough to share.

## 4. Data processing

The raw tables were reshaped into one long table (`02_clean_psa.py`) and then into a monthly regional panel (`03_build_panel.py`). The key processing decisions:

- **Choosing the shock (D5).** Without the reference study's database, the PSA fuel sub-index (07.2.2) was chosen as the retail fuel price. It was validated before use: its monthly changes correlate 0.87 with the World Bank's Manila pump price and 0.99 with PSA's gasoline-only index. It also covers 1994–2026, every region and the 2026 shock.
- **Geography (D6).** PSA's bottom-30% backcasts exist only on the old geographic code (17 regions). The panel uses that code; January–August 2026, published only on the new code, is chained from new-code monthly changes, and the four regions whose boundaries changed are flagged.
- **Splicing base years (D7).** The bottom-30% series joins PSA's own 2018-base backcasts at January 2012 and January 2018. The joins were checked against the older bases (monthly changes correlate perfectly over 2012–2017; the January 2018 jump matches the TRAIN tax reform). The largest month-to-month moves in the gap turned out to be the 2008 rice crisis, a real event.
- **A date-alignment bug.** The World Bank fuel file dates months by the day of collection (e.g., 3 January 2017) rather than the first of the month, so its series did not line up with the monthly calendar and a robustness regression failed. Fixed by converting all dates to month starts. The same check showed the Philippine series starts in January 2017, not December 2015.

## 5. Validation before estimation

Two safeguards were built before any Philippine estimate was produced.

**A simulation test.** Data are generated from a model whose impulse response is known; the estimator must recover it. Five automated tests (national and panel, three kinds of standard errors, cumulative responses, group differences) pass in every run.

**A pre-registered replication gate.** Criteria were written into the decision log before running: on EU data, the impact response to after-tax pump prices had to lie between 0.035 and 0.075 (reference 0.055) and fade by the second month; the after-tax response had to exceed the before-tax and crude-oil responses; and the EU fuels index had to give the advanced-economy shape. Results: {{ n.rep_after }}, {{ n.rep_before }} and {{ n.rep_brent }} against reference values 0.055, 0.020 and 0.015. All three tests passed. Writing the criteria first matters: it prevents adjusting the test to fit the results.

## 6. The estimation plan

The full plan for RQ1–RQ4 and twelve robustness checks was written into the decision log (D8) before the first Philippine estimate. This is a form of pre-registration: it shows which results were planned and which were discovered.

## 7. Results and how they were interpreted

**RQ1.** Pass-through is fast and persistent ({{ n.rq1_all_c0 }} on impact, {{ n.rq1_all_c12 }} after a year). This matched expectations.

**RQ2.** The gap was negative — the opposite of the reference study's progressive finding. Rather than accept or reject this at once, it was tested against every planned robustness check. The sign held throughout, but three checks shrank it toward zero.

**RQ3 and a puzzle.** Combining the division responses with the two baskets' weights predicted a *progressive* gap (+0.010), while the direct estimate was regressive. The two could only be reconciled if the poor face different price changes *within* categories. This led to a supplementary analysis (RQ3b, D9) using PSA's bottom-30% component indices, which confirmed it: the poor's utilities and food prices respond more, their transport (mainly fares) less. This is one of the study's main contributions, and it was found by taking an inconsistency seriously rather than ignoring it.

**A bug found by checking the output.** The first run of RQ4 used the food weights in PSA's regional weight tables, which turned out to be each region's share of the *national* basket (NCR 6.0, CAR 0.6), essentially region size. The planned variable was food's share *within* each region's basket. The bug was fixed (food share now 29–59%) before any result was reported, and recorded in D9.

**Sub-periods.** Splitting the sample showed the regressive effect concentrated in 2001–2012 ({{ n.split_p1 }}) and weaker since 2013 ({{ n.split_p2 }}). This explains why the short alternative fuel series (2018 onward) give values near zero, and it is reported openly.

**RQ4.** Regional interactions pointed in the hypothesised direction for Mindanao and island regions but were imprecise, and none survived a multiple-testing correction. The food-share interaction ran the other way; the descriptive analysis later explained why (Section 10).

## 8. The 2026 extension

You asked whether the study captures the 2026 shock and can support evidence-based policy. The main model uses 2026 data only at short horizons, so four supplementary analyses were specified in the decision log before being run (D10), and a fifth, descriptive one was added afterwards:

- **E1:** a model estimated only on data to December 2025 predicts the fuel-driven part of the 2026 rise. Result: about half of the actual rise, and the right sign of the gap.
- **E2–E3:** the same by region, converted to pesos for a poverty-line family of five (PHP {{ n.e3_ph_cost }} a month nationally).
- **E4:** responses of household fuels, fares and cereals in both baskets. Result: LPG responds equally in both, kerosene strongly but with a tiny weight, charcoal and wood not at all.
- **E5:** a decomposition of the actual 2026 gap by item. Result: rice and cereals contributed {{ n.e5_cereals_gap }} pp of a {{ n.e5_total_gap }} pp gap.

**Policy research.** The 2026 policy response was researched online. RA 12316 was verified from the law text. Executive orders, subsidy amounts and fare orders came from news and government press sources and are marked "secondary" in Appendix D, because several official pages could not be fetched; they should be checked against the issuances before submission. The rice finding was checked against reports that attribute 2026 rice price increases to fertilizer and freight costs and to import policy.

**Reconciling two readings.** The model attributes about half of the 2026 gap to fuel; the decomposition attributes most of it to rice. Both can hold if rice is one route by which oil reaches the poor (fertilizer, freight), a route the post-2013 data do not show for pump prices. The paper states this rather than choosing one reading.

## 10. Descriptive analysis (Objective 1)

For the working paper's first objective, a descriptive analysis was added (D11): summary statistics by period, annual inflation, fuel-shock episodes, cross-correlations, regional summaries and unit-root tests. Two findings shaped the interpretation of the model results. First, the poor's inflation exceeded the average in {{ n.d_episodes_b30_higher }} of {{ n.d_n_episodes }} fuel-shock episodes and in {{ n.d_years_b30_higher }} of {{ n.d_years_total }} years. Second, the cumulative gap is *smaller* in poorer, food-dependent regions — because where most households are poor, the all-income basket resembles the poor's. This explains the counter-intuitive food-share interaction in RQ4. The unit-root tests confirm that price levels are non-stationary and their changes stationary, the formal reason for estimating in changes.

## 11. Figures

Figures were first drawn in colour and then converted to black and white for journal printing: series are distinguished by line style and marker shape, confidence bands by grey fill or dotted lines, bars by grey fill or hatching; the font is serif; and titles were removed from the images because journals place captions in the text. Each figure is saved as 300-dpi PNG and TIFF and as vector PDF. ## Lessons for doing this kind of study

- **Validate the estimator before using it**, on simulated data and on published results, with criteria written in advance.
- **Write the plan before seeing results**, and label everything added afterwards.
- **Check intermediate output, not just final numbers.** Three of the errors found in this study (missing regions, misaligned dates, the wrong food weight) were caught by looking at counts, coverage and magnitudes.
- **Take inconsistencies seriously.** The contradiction between the weight decomposition and the direct estimate led to the study's main mechanism.
- **Separate verified from unverified facts** and say which is which.
- **Generate the text from the results**, so that every number in the paper can be traced to its source.

## Glossary of methods

- **Local projection:** estimating the effect of a shock at each horizon with its own regression (Chapter 3 of the working paper).
- **Teulings–Zubanov correction:** including the shocks between the impact date and the horizon, so the coefficient measures a one-time shock.
- **Cumulative response:** effect on the price level after *h* months; the sum of per-horizon effects.
- **Newey–West standard errors:** allow for serial correlation in a single time series.
- **Driscoll–Kraay standard errors:** allow for correlation across regions and over time in a panel.
- **Augmented Dickey–Fuller test:** tests whether a series has a unit root (is non-stationary).
- **Fixed-base (Laspeyres) decomposition:** splits a price index change into item contributions using fixed weights.
- **Pre-registration:** writing down the analysis plan and pass/fail criteria before seeing results.
- **Bonferroni correction:** dividing the significance level by the number of tests to limit false positives.
